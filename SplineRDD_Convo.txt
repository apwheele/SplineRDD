      103  
      104 +## Kernel estimator                                           
      105 +                                                              
      106 +The kernel estimator is rdrobust with its defaults [@calonico2
          +017rdrobust], using the Python version of the package. It fits
          + a local linear regression on each side of the cutoff with tri
          +angular kernel weights, $w_i = 1 - |x_i - c|/h$ for cases with
          +in the bandwidth $h$ and zero otherwise. The bandwidth is the 
          +same on both sides and is chosen to minimize the mean squared 
          +error of the estimate [@calonico2020optimal]. The point estima
          +te is the difference between the two intercepts at the cutoff.
          + The confidence interval is the robust bias-corrected interval
          +. It subtracts an estimate of the bias, taken from a local qua
          +dratic fit with a second and larger bandwidth, and widens the 
          +standard error to account for that step [@calonico2014robust; 
          +@calonico2018effect]. This is the interval the rdrobust author
          +s recommend reporting.                                        
      107 +                                                              
      108 +## Spline models                                              
      109 +                                                              
      110 +The spline models are fit by ordinary least squares to all of 
          +the data. The basis is a natural cubic spline, also called a r
          +estricted cubic spline. It is a cubic polynomial between knots
          +, joined smoothly at the knots, and constrained to be linear b
          +eyond the two outer knots [@durrleman1989flexible; @harrell201
          +5regression]. The outer knots are the smallest and largest val
          +ues of $x$, and the interior knots are on a regular grid with 
          +spacing $s$. I consider spacings of 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, 1/6, 1
          +/8, 1/10, and 1/12 of the range of $x$, and pick the spacing w
          +ith the lowest leave-one-out mean squared error. For least squ
          +ares the leave-one-out residual has a closed form, $e_i / (1 -
          + h_{ii})$ with $h_{ii}$ the leverage, so the search costs almo
          +st nothing. Interior knots closer than $s/2$ to the outer knot
          +s are dropped.                                                
      111 +                                                              
      112 +The four spline models differ in where the knots go and how mu
          +ch the curve may change at the cutoff. The first, *spline with
          + the cutoff between knots*, is one spline over the whole range
          + plus the dummy $D$. The knot grid is shifted so the cutoff fa
          +lls halfway between two knots, at $c \pm s/2, c \pm 3s/2$, and
          + so on, and the effect is the coefficient on $D$. This is the 
          +model in @wheeler2021rdd. The second, *spline with a knot at t
          +he cutoff*, is the same except the grid is $c, c \pm s, c \pm 
          +2s, \ldots$, so one knot sits on the cutoff. The third adds $D
          +(x - c)$ and $D(x - c)^2$ to the knot-at-cutoff model, so the 
          +slope and the curvature of the curve may change at the cutoff.
          + The fourth fits a separate cubic B-spline to each side of the
          + cutoff, with knots on the same grid. That is the spline versi
          +on of fitting separate lines or polynomials on each side.     
      113 +                                                              
      114 +The first two models assume the curve has the same shape on bo
          +th sides of the cutoff, and the treatment shifts it up or down
          +. That is a strong assumption. If the treatment effect varies 
          +with $x$, the two sides will have different slopes, and a comm
          +on spline has to bend to fit both. The last two models relax t
          +he assumption. For the separate splines I do not use the natur
          +al spline basis. A natural spline has zero curvature at its ou
          +ter knots, and with separate fits the outer knot on each side 
          +is the cutoff, which is exactly where the estimate is taken. I
          +n early runs that constraint biased the separate natural splin
          +es the same way a straight line is biased. A plain cubic B-spl
          +ine has no constraint at the cutoff.

      117 +                                                              
      118 +## Tree models                                                
      119 +                                                              
      120 +The random forest [@breiman2001random; @pedregosa2011scikit] a
          +nd XGBoost [@chen2016xgboost] are fit separately to each side 
          +of the cutoff, what @kunzel2019metalearners call a T-learner. 
          +The effect is the treated model's prediction at the cutoff min
          +us the control model's prediction at the cutoff. The random fo
          +rest has 100 trees and picks its minimum leaf size (1%, 2.5%, 
          +5%, or 10% of the cases on that side, and at least 3) by out-o
          +f-bag error. XGBoost uses trees of depth 2, a learning rate of
          + 0.05, 80% subsampling, and a minimum child weight of 5, and p
          +icks the number of rounds (10 to 400) by 5-fold cross-validati
          +on. The only feature is $x$.                                  
      121 +                                                              
      122 +Tree models predict a constant beyond their last split. So at 
          +the cutoff they average the outcomes of the nearest cases on e
          +ach side, which is a kernel estimator that fits a flat line in
          +stead of a sloped one. That has a bias proportional to the slo
          +pe of the curve at the cutoff [@imbens2008regression]. I inclu
          +de the tree models because the question of how to get a counte
          +rfactual out of a machine learning model comes up often, not b
          +ecause I expect them to do well.                              
      123 +                                                              
      124 +## Out-of-sample error                                        
      125 +                                                              
      126 +Every model gets an out-of-sample prediction for every case. F
          +or the splines these are exact leave-one-out predictions from 
          +the hat matrix. The kernel estimator is two weighted least squ
          +ares lines, so its leave-one-out predictions also have a close
          +d form, but only for the cases within the bandwidth. The rando
          +m forest uses out-of-bag predictions and XGBoost uses 5-fold c
          +ross-validation, since refitting them for every case would be 
          +too slow. To compare models, I take the mean squared out-of-sa
          +mple error of the cases within the kernel estimator's bandwidt
          +h, weighted by the triangular kernel weights, so the cases clo
          +sest to the cutoff count the most. This is meant to give the o
          +ut-of-sample comparison its best shot at measuring fit near th
          +e cutoff. I report it divided by $\sigma^2$, so 1 is the error
          + of a model that knows the true curve.                        
      127 +                                                              
      128 +## Conformal intervals for the counterfactual                 
      129 +                                                              
      130 +For the models whose control side is fit only to control cases
          + (the separate splines, the random forest, and XGBoost), the c
          +ontrol model's prediction for a treated case is a counterfactu
          +al, what that case's outcome would have been without treatment
          +. For the $m$ treated cases within the bandwidth of the cutoff
          +, the estimated individual effect is $e_i = y_i - \hat{f}_0(x_
          +i)$.                                                          
      131 +                                                              
      132 +A conformal prediction interval puts a margin around a predict
          +ion equal to a quantile of the out-of-sample errors [@vovk2005
          +algorithmic; @lei2018distribution]. Using leave-one-out errors
          + gives the jackknife version [@barber2021predictive], and out-
          +of-bag errors the jackknife+-after-bootstrap version [@kim2020
          +predictive]. I build three intervals from the out-of-sample er
          +rors $r_j$ of the $n_0$ control cases. The *individual* interv
          +al is $e_i \pm q$, where $q$ is the $\lceil 0.95(n_0 + 1) \rce
          +il$-th smallest $|r_j|$. This is a prediction interval for eac
          +h treated case's own effect, in the spirit of @lei2021conforma
          +l. The *average* interval is $\bar{e} \pm q_m$, where $q_m$ is
          + the same quantile taken over the absolute means of every run 
          +of $m$ consecutive control residuals, sorted by $x$. It treats
          + the average of $m$ out-of-sample errors as the reference dist
          +ribution for the average of the $m$ counterfactual errors.    
      133 +                                                              
      134 +The standard out-of-sample errors are mostly interpolation err
          +ors, the model predicting a case with data on both sides of it
          +. The counterfactual is an extrapolation across the cutoff. So
          + the third interval, the *rolling origin* interval, refits the
          + control model at 20 pseudo-cutoffs on the control side. Each 
          +time it uses only the control cases to the left of the pseudo-
          +cutoff, keeps the tuning from the full fit, and predicts the c
          +ontrol cases within $h$ to the right of the pseudo-cutoff. The
          + pseudo-cutoffs run from $c - h$ down to the point that leaves
          + at least 20 cases (or 25% of the control cases, whichever is 
          +more) to fit on. The interval is $\bar{e} \pm q_{ro}$, with $q
          +_{ro}$ the conformal quantile of the 20 absolute block mean er
          +rors. This is the regression discontinuity version of the forw
          +ard-forecast conformal intervals I used for synthetic control 
          +designs [@wheeler2026synthpower], and it is close to the one-s
          +ided cross-validation that @imbens2008regression suggested for
          + choosing a bandwidth.                                        
      135 +                                                              
      136 +Every treated case has the same effect in the simulations belo
          +w, so an interval covers if it contains $\tau$.

      322 -DISCUSSION                                                    
      322 +I went into this expecting to show that kernel estimators for 
          +RDD are junk. That is not what the simulations show. What they
          + show is a trade-off, and which side of it you are on depends 
          +on an assumption you cannot check very well with the data.    
      323  
      324 +If the outcome curve has the same shape on both sides of the c
          +utoff, so the treatment just shifts it up or down, a regressio
          +n spline with a dummy variable at the cutoff is much better th
          +an the kernel estimator. It is about as accurate as the kernel
          + estimator with twice the data, its intervals are less than ha
          +lf as wide, and it can detect effects about half the size. The
          + kernel estimator's main problem in those designs is that it o
          +nly uses the cases near the cutoff, and its bias-corrected int
          +erval is built to be safe when the curve is bent, so it is wid
          +er than it needs to be when the curve is not. With 100 cases t
          +he kernel estimator is also erratic, with a few estimates far 
          +off and some samples where it cannot produce an estimate at al
          +l. That part of my skepticism holds up.                       
      325 +                                                              
      326 +If the curve changes shape at the cutoff, as it does in the tw
          +o benchmark designs built from real data [@lee2008randomized; 
          +@ludwig2007does], the common spline is biased, the bias does n
          +ot go away with more data, and its intervals miss the true eff
          +ect far more often than 5% of the time. How biased it is depen
          +ds on where the knots happen to fall relative to the kink, whi
          +ch is the same kind of fragility I complained about for kernel
          + bandwidths. The kernel estimator handles that case better, si
          +nce it fits a separate line on each side, although its coverag
          +e in the Lee design still falls short as the sample grows. The
          + splines that allow the shape to change at the cutoff do about
          + as well as the kernel estimator there, and give up much of th
          +e efficiency advantage in the smooth designs. So the choice is
          + less about kernels versus splines than about what you assume 
          +about the curve at the cutoff. A spline that lets the slope an
          +d curvature change at the cutoff sits between the two.        
      327 +                                                              
      328 +The assumption of a common shape is a statement about the trea
          +tment effect, not just the curve. If the effect of the treatme
          +nt varies with the running variable, the two sides will have d
          +ifferent slopes even if the untreated outcome is perfectly smo
          +oth. In criminology that is plausible for many treatments assi
          +gned by a score. A program assigned by a risk score could easi
          +ly do more for people well above the cutoff than for people ju
          +st above it, and then the treated side bends away from the unt
          +reated side. When the effect is a simple shift, for example a 
          +fixed fine or a sentence enhancement applied at a threshold, t
          +he common spline is more defensible.                          
      329 +                                                              
      330 +My practical advice is to fit both. Estimate the common spline
          + model and a model that lets the two sides differ (the kernel 
          +estimator, or a spline with a slope and curvature change), and
          + plot both fits. If they agree, report the common spline, with
          + its shorter interval. If they disagree, the shape change at t
          +he cutoff matters, and the common spline should not be trusted
          +. I would also fix the knot spacing ahead of time rather than 
          +choosing it by cross-validation, since choosing it from the da
          +ta made the intervals too narrow.                             
      331 +                                                              
      332 +That leads to the out-of-sample question. I hoped cross-valida
          +ted prediction error could settle which model to trust in a gi
          +ven dataset, and it cannot. The differences in prediction erro
          +r between models are tiny compared with the noise, because the
          + effect estimate depends on the fitted curve at one point and 
          +the prediction error averages over all the cases. Averaged ove
          +r many samples, out-of-sample error caught the large shape cha
          +nge in the Ludwig-Miller design but ranked the models backward
          +s in the Lee design. Within one sample it picked the most accu
          +rate model about as often as chance. Validation aimed at the c
          +utoff is a better idea. @imbens2008regression suggested one-si
          +ded cross-validation that predicts each case from the cases on
          + the far side of it, and @kettlewell2022optimal choose among R
          +DD specifications by their estimates at placebo cutoffs, where
          + the true effect is zero. Both target the extrapolation to a b
          +oundary that the effect estimate depends on. But neither can s
          +ee a shape change that happens only at the real cutoff, which 
          +is the case where the common spline fails.

      535  That leads to the out-of-sample question. I hoped cross-valida
           ted prediction error could settle which model to trust in a gi
           ven dataset, and it cannot. The differences in prediction erro
           r between models are tiny compared with the noise, because the
            effect estimate depends on the fitted curve at one point and 
           the prediction error averages over all the cases. Averaged ove
           r many samples, out-of-sample error caught the large shape cha

            the true effect is zero. Both target the extrapolation to a b

           ee a shape change that happens only at the real cutoff, which

  What the simulations show (effects in noise-SD units):
  - Same curve shape on both sides of the cutoff: the spline with a dummy wins
    clearly. rdrobust needs 1.6 to 2.2 times as much data to match it. With
    knots fixed in advance, the spline's intervals cover 95% and are 25–40%
    shorter.
  - Small samples: at n=100 rdrobust is erratic. 2–6% of its estimates are off
    by more than 3σ, and in 4–5% of Lee and Ludwig–Miller samples it gives no
    estimate at all.
  - Shape changes at the cutoff (the Lee and Ludwig–Miller benchmarks used to
    develop rdrobust): the common spline is biased, and its intervals cover the
    true effect as little as 60% of the time. Splines that let the slope and
    curvature change do about as well as rdrobust.
  - Your knot ideas: picking knot spacing by leave-one-out makes intervals too
    narrow (about 91% coverage); fixing it in advance fixes that. But in the
    shape-change designs the bias depends on where the knots happen to fall, the
    same fragility you criticized in bandwidths.
  - Out-of-sample error as a model selector: it doesn't work well. The
    differences between models are tiny next to the noise. Within one sample it
    picks the most accurate model about as often as chance, and it ranks the
    models backwards in the Lee design.
  - Random forest and XGBoost: both are biased at the cutoff, because trees
    extrapolate flat. Individual conformal intervals cover but are about 4σ
    wide. Average-effect intervals built from leave-one-out residuals cover 0–9%
    of the time. Rolling-origin intervals reach 89–93% when the shape doesn't
    change, and fail completely when it does.

  Hansen application:
  - Full data: rdrobust can't tell the 0.08 effect from zero with 124,000 stops;
    its interval runs from -3.2 to +0.9 points. The splines and Hansen's own
    fixed-window regression find about -1.4 to -2.2 points, with intervals that
    exclude zero.

    splines 43–46%, and Hansen's regression 61%.
  - Fake cutoff at 0.05: the common splines reject 22% of the time, because the
    recidivism curve bends there. This is a real-data example of their weakness.
